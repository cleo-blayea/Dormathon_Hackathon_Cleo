**Agent's independent thermal analysis:**

The temperature curve shows three distinct phases:
1. **Startup phase (11:17:00)**: Coolant jumped from 44.8°C to 84.8°C in the first 30 seconds — this is the initial heat soak when the engine reaches operating temperature.
2. **Plateau phase (11:17:30–11:29:30)**: Temperature stabilized around 84–89°C — this is normal operating range for a loaded diesel.
3. **Climbing phase (11:31:30–12:03:30)**: Temperature began climbing steadily at ~1.3°C/min without any load change — this is abnormal. In a properly functioning cooling system, temperature should stabilize. The fact that it never plateaued at a second equilibrium point indicates the cooling system's heat dissipation capacity was insufficient for the current load.

The oil pressure decline correlates with rising temperature (oil viscosity decreases with temperature), but the 1.27 bar decline (from 4.54 to 3.27) is more than typical thermal thinning would explain alone. This may indicate an oil cooler efficiency problem (if the oil cooler shares the cooling circuit).

**Agent's observation the script did not make:**

In the first scenario (Normal Operation), the generator ran at 165.6 kW (60% load) for 30 minutes and coolant only reached 70.6°C — well within normal range. In the overheating scenario, load was 192 kW (70% — only 10% higher) but the cooling system failed to maintain temperature. This suggests the cooling system may be marginal — it can handle moderate loads but not sustained higher loads. A derating assessment may be needed.

**Likely causes (ranked by probability):**

1. **Blocked or fouled radiator** — The last maintenance included "radiator cleaning," but the cleaning may have been superficial. Gradual temperature rise is the classic signature of insufficient heat dissipation.
2. **Thermostat stuck partially closed** — Would restrict coolant flow, causing gradual overheating. Temperature would climb steadily rather than spike.
3. **Water pump efficiency degradation** — Impeller wear or corrosion reducing flow. Would produce the same gradual rise pattern.
4. **Coolant degradation** — Old or diluted coolant has reduced heat transfer capacity. Coolant quality was not checked in the last service.
5. **Engine room ventilation** — If ambient temperature in the generator room is high, the radiator cannot dissipate heat effectively even if mechanically clean.

**Confidence:** High that the cooling system is failing to dissipate heat adequately under sustained load. Medium on the specific component — radiator blockage and thermostat failure are the top candidates. Low on water pump failure (would typically produce a faster temperature rise or fluctuation).

**Required actions (ordered):**

1. **Pressure-test the cooling system** and check for leaks.
2. **Inspect radiator** — remove and professionally clean if necessary. Check for bent fins, debris, or internal blockage.
3. **Test thermostat** — remove and test in hot water. Replace if it does not open fully at the rated temperature.
4. **Check coolant quality** — test freeze point, pH, and contamination. Replace if degraded.
5. **Inspect water pump** — check impeller condition and weep hole for leakage.
6. **Assess engine room ventilation** — verify adequate airflow for standby rating.
7. **Perform a sustained load test** at 70% load for 60 minutes with continuous temperature monitoring. Verify the temperature plateaus at a stable value.
8. **Obtain DSE6120 alarm configuration** — confirm actual coolant temperature alarm and shutdown thresholds.

**Estimated urgency:** Must be completed before any expected sustained outage. If a real outage occurs and the generator overheats and shuts down, the facility has no backup power.
